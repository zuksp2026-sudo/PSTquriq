PST as a Categorical Functorial Trajectory

Spartac Cebanu
November 17, 2026

Abstract

This document gives a corrected categorical formulation of the PST process structure

$$ Q_1\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow Q_2. $$

If PST is defined as a functorial trajectory, its operator pattern is

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o, $$

read left-to-right as a sequence of functor applications. Equivalently, the final PST state is given by the functor composite

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$

The document also states precisely what follows from the adjunction

$$ \Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z $$

and what requires separate PST axioms. This document treats PST as a functorial model. Determinism is not implied by functoriality alone and requires additional assumptions on the categories, functors, initial state, and absence of nondeterministic choices.

Purpose

This document gives a corrected categorical formulation of the PST process structure

$$ Q_1\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow Q_2. $$

The central result is that, if PST is defined as a functorial trajectory, then its operator pattern is

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o, $$

read left-to-right as a sequence of functor applications. Equivalently, the final PST state is given by the functor composite

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$

The document also states precisely what follows from the adjunction

$$ \Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z $$

and what requires separate PST axioms.

Basic distinction

In ordinary category theory, a morphism exists only inside one category. Therefore, if \(\mathcal O\) and \(\mathcal P\) are distinct categories, an expression such as

$$ Q_1\to U:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P $$

is not a morphism in either category.

There are two correct ways to formalize PST:

  1. Typed-graph PST: the arrows are formal typed transitions between objects of different categories.
  2. Functorial PST: the arrows are applications of functors between \(\mathcal O\) and \(\mathcal P\).

This document uses the second, stronger formulation.

PST categories and functors

Definition (PST categories).

Let \(\mathcal O\) be the category of objects: material configurations, structures, or accumulated states.

Let \(\mathcal P\) be the category of projections: gradients, vectors, functional descriptions, or dynamical representations.

Definition (PST functors).

Let

$$ \Phi_z:\mathcal O\longrightarrow\mathcal P $$

be the forgetting functor, which extracts a projection from an object.

Let

$$ \Phi_o:\mathcal P\longrightarrow\mathcal O $$

be the freeing functor, which constructs an object from a projection.

Remark.

The notation is chosen as follows:

$$ \Phi_z=\text{forgetting functor}, \qquad \Phi_o=\text{freeing functor}. $$

Thus:

$$ \Phi_z:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P, \qquad \Phi_o:\mathcal P\to\mathcal O. $$
Remark (Sequential PST notation).

Throughout this document, the semicolon notation

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o $$

denotes left-to-right sequential application of functors.

Thus, for an object \(X\in\mathcal O\),

$$ (\Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o)(X) = \Phi_o(\Phi_z(\Phi_o(\Phi_z(X)))). $$

Equivalently, in ordinary functor-composition notation,

$$ (\Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o)(X) = (\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(X). $$

PST as a functorial trajectory

Axiom (PST functorial trajectory).

A PST process is a directed chain

$$ Q_1\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow Q_2 $$

defined by the following functorial equations:

$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1), $$ $$ R=\Phi_o(U), $$ $$ I=\Phi_z(R), $$ $$ Q_2=\Phi_o(I). $$
Remark.

This axiom is stronger than merely saying that \(Q_1,R,Q_2\in\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal O)\) and \(U,I\in\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal P)\). It says that each node is obtained from the previous one by a fixed functor. Therefore, the PST chain is not merely a typed graph; it is a functorial trajectory.

Lemma (Node typing).

In a PST functorial trajectory,

$$ Q_1,R,Q_2\in\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal O), \qquad U,I\in\operatorname{Ob}(\mathcal P). $$
Proof.

Since \(\Phi_z:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P\) and \(Q_1\in\mathcal O\), we have

$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1)\in\mathcal P. $$

Since \(\Phi_o:\mathcal P\to\mathcal O\) and \(U\in\mathcal P\), we have

$$ R=\Phi_o(U)\in\mathcal O. $$

Since \(\Phi_z:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P\) and \(R\in\mathcal O\), we have

$$ I=\Phi_z(R)\in\mathcal P. $$

Since \(\Phi_o:\mathcal P\to\mathcal O\) and \(I\in\mathcal P\), we have

$$ Q_2=\Phi_o(I)\in\mathcal O. $$

Therefore:

$$ Q_1,R,Q_2\in\mathcal O, \qquad U,I\in\mathcal P. $$
Corollary (PST node-type alternation).

Every PST functorial trajectory has node types

$$ \mathsf O-\mathsf P-\mathsf O-\mathsf P-\mathsf O. $$
Proof.

By the node-typing lemma,

$$ Q_1,R,Q_2\in\mathcal O, \qquad U,I\in\mathcal P. $$

Therefore:

$$ \operatorname{type}(Q_1)=\mathsf O, \qquad \operatorname{type}(U)=\mathsf P, \qquad \operatorname{type}(R)=\mathsf O, \qquad \operatorname{type}(I)=\mathsf P, \qquad \operatorname{type}(Q_2)=\mathsf O. $$

Hence:

$$ Q_1-U-R-I-Q_2 = \mathsf O-\mathsf P-\mathsf O-\mathsf P-\mathsf O. $$

PST composition theorem

Theorem (PST functor-composition theorem).

Assume that PST is a functorial trajectory:

$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1), \qquad R=\Phi_o(U), \qquad I=\Phi_z(R), \qquad Q_2=\Phi_o(I). $$

Then

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$
Proof.

By the PST functorial-trajectory axiom,

$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1). $$

Therefore,

$$ R=\Phi_o(U)=\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1)). $$

Therefore,

$$ I=\Phi_z(R)=\Phi_z(\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1))). $$

Therefore,

$$ Q_2=\Phi_o(I) = \Phi_o(\Phi_z(\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1)))). $$

Using functor composition, this is

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$
Theorem (PST operator-pattern theorem).

Assume that PST is a functorial trajectory. Then the operator pattern of PST is the sequence of functor applications

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o. $$

Equivalently, the PST chain is generated by the alternating application

$$ \Phi_z,\qquad\Phi_o,\qquad\Phi_z,\qquad\Phi_o. $$
Proof.

By the PST functorial-trajectory axiom,

$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1), $$ $$ R=\Phi_o(U), $$ $$ I=\Phi_z(R), $$ $$ Q_2=\Phi_o(I). $$

Therefore, the first transition is the application of \(\Phi_z\) to \(Q_1\):

$$ Q_1\mapsto U=\Phi_z(Q_1). $$

The second transition is the application of \(\Phi_o\) to \(U\):

$$ U\mapsto R=\Phi_o(U). $$

The third transition is the application of \(\Phi_z\) to \(R\):

$$ R\mapsto I=\Phi_z(R). $$

The fourth transition is the application of \(\Phi_o\) to \(I\):

$$ I\mapsto Q_2=\Phi_o(I). $$

Hence, the PST process has operator pattern

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o. $$
Remark (Correct reading of the PST chain).

The expression

$$ Q_1\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow Q_2 $$

is a chain of object-to-object transitions.

The expression

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o $$

is the sequence of functors generating those transitions.

Therefore, the following expression is not a categorical identity between objects and functors:

$$ Q_1\longrightarrow U\longrightarrow R\longrightarrow I\longrightarrow Q_2 = \Phi_z\longrightarrow\Phi_o\longrightarrow\Phi_z\longrightarrow\Phi_o. $$

The correct statement is:

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$

Alternatively, one may say that the PST chain has operator pattern

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o. $$

Adjunction and PST

Definition (Adjunction).

Let

$$ \Phi_o:\mathcal P\to\mathcal O, \qquad \Phi_z:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P. $$

An adjunction

$$ \Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z $$

consists of natural transformations

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}, $$

such that the triangle identities hold.

Proposition (Unit and counit).

If \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\), then:

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$
Proof.

This is part of the definition of an adjunction. For an adjunction \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\), the unit is

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$

and the counit is

$$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$

Thus \(\eta\) and \(\varepsilon\) are canonical natural transformations associated with the adjunction.

Proposition (Triangle identities).

If \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\), then for every \(P\in\mathcal P\) and \(O\in\mathcal O\),

$$ \varepsilon_{\Phi_o(P)} \circ \Phi_o(\eta_P) = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_o(P)}, $$ $$ \Phi_z(\varepsilon_O) \circ \eta_{\Phi_z(O)} = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_z(O)}. $$
Proof.

These are the standard triangle identities of an adjunction. For an adjunction \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\), the unit and counit satisfy:

$$ (\varepsilon\Phi_o)\circ(\Phi_o\eta) = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_o}, $$ $$ (\Phi_z\varepsilon)\circ(\eta\Phi_z) = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_z}. $$

Equivalently, for every \(P\in\mathcal P\) and \(O\in\mathcal O\):

$$ \varepsilon_{\Phi_o(P)} \circ \Phi_o(\eta_P) = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_o(P)}, $$ $$ \Phi_z(\varepsilon_O) \circ \eta_{\Phi_z(O)} = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_z(O)}. $$
Proposition (Hom-set bijection).

If \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\), then for every \(P\in\mathcal P\) and \(O\in\mathcal O\) there is a natural bijection

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)). $$
Proof.

By the hom-set definition of adjunction, \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\) means that for every \(P\in\mathcal P\) and \(O\in\mathcal O\) there is a natural bijection

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)). $$

This is the standard form:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal D}(F(X),Y) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal C}(X,G(Y)), $$

where

$$ F=\Phi_o:\mathcal P\to\mathcal O, \qquad G=\Phi_z:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P. $$

Therefore:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)). $$
Remark (Correct PST substitution).

Taking \(P=U\) and \(O=Q_1\), the Hom-set bijection gives:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(U),Q_1) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(U,\Phi_z(Q_1)). $$

Since \(R=\Phi_o(U)\), this becomes:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(R,Q_1) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(U,\Phi_z(Q_1)). $$

Since \(\Phi_z(Q_1)=U\), this may also be written as:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(R,Q_1) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(U,U). $$

This is a formal Hom-set correspondence; it does not by itself assert a physical current or a causal relation from \(R\) to \(Q_1\).

Taking \(P=I\) and \(O=R\), the Hom-set bijection gives:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(I),R) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(I,\Phi_z(R)). $$

Since \(Q_2=\Phi_o(I)\), this becomes:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(Q_2,R) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(I,\Phi_z(R)). $$

Since \(I=\Phi_z(R)\), this may also be written as:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(Q_2,R) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(I,I). $$

Thus the correct PST Hom-correspondences are:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(R,Q_1) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(U,U), $$ $$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(Q_2,R) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(I,I). $$

Epsilon, eta, and PST edges

Remark (Level distinction).

The operators \(\eta\) and \(\varepsilon\) are natural transformations:

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$

Therefore, they are not morphisms of the PST chain and cannot be identified with its edges.

The PST chain consists of applications of functors:

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o. $$

The operators \(\eta\) and \(\varepsilon\) live between composites of these functors.

Remark (PST operator assignment).

In PST, the following assignment is used as a labeling convention:

$$ \varepsilon\leftrightarrow\Phi_z, \qquad \eta\leftrightarrow\Phi_o. $$

This means:

  • \(\varepsilon\) is associated with the forgetting functor \(\Phi_z\).
  • \(\eta\) is associated with the freeing functor \(\Phi_o\).

However, categorically, both \(\eta\) and \(\varepsilon\) depend on both functors:

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$

Therefore, the PST assignment is a mnemonic or labeling convention, not a categorical identity.

Adjunction data along a PST trajectory

Theorem (Adjunction data along a PST trajectory).

Assume that PST is a functorial trajectory and that

$$ \Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z. $$

Then the adjunction provides canonical natural transformations

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$

Moreover, for every \(P\in\mathcal P\) and \(O\in\mathcal O\),

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)). $$

In particular, along the PST trajectory, the adjunction provides the components

$$ \eta_U:U\longrightarrow\Phi_z(\Phi_o(U)), $$ $$ \varepsilon_R:\Phi_o(\Phi_z(R))\longrightarrow R, $$ $$ \eta_I:I\longrightarrow\Phi_z(\Phi_o(I)), $$ $$ \varepsilon_{Q_1}:\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1))\longrightarrow Q_1. $$

Since \(R=\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1))\), the counit component at \(Q_1\) is

$$ \varepsilon_{Q_1}:R\longrightarrow Q_1. $$
Proof.

By assumption, \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\). Therefore, there exist canonical natural transformations

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$

These satisfy the triangle identities:

$$ \varepsilon_{\Phi_o(P)} \circ \Phi_o(\eta_P) = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_o(P)}, $$ $$ \Phi_z(\varepsilon_O) \circ \eta_{\Phi_z(O)} = \operatorname{id}_{\Phi_z(O)}. $$

Therefore, the adjunction gives the natural Hom-set bijection

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)). $$

Since \(U,I\in\mathcal P\) and \(Q_1,R,Q_2\in\mathcal O\), the adjunction components are available at the PST states:

$$ \eta_U:U\longrightarrow\Phi_z(\Phi_o(U)), $$ $$ \varepsilon_R:\Phi_o(\Phi_z(R))\longrightarrow R, $$ $$ \eta_I:I\longrightarrow\Phi_z(\Phi_o(I)), $$ $$ \varepsilon_{Q_1}:\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1))\longrightarrow Q_1. $$

Because \(R=\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1))\), we obtain

$$ \varepsilon_{Q_1}:R\longrightarrow Q_1. $$

Main result

Theorem (Final PST categorical theorem).

Assume:

  1. \(\mathcal O\) and \(\mathcal P\) are categories.
  2. \(\Phi_z:\mathcal O\to\mathcal P\) and \(\Phi_o:\mathcal P\to\mathcal O\) are functors.
  3. A PST process is a functorial trajectory:
$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1), \qquad R=\Phi_o(U), \qquad I=\Phi_z(R), \qquad Q_2=\Phi_o(I). $$
  1. \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\) is an adjunction.

Then:

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$

Furthermore, the PST process has operator pattern

$$ \Phi_z;\Phi_o;\Phi_z;\Phi_o. $$

Moreover, the adjunction provides:

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}, $$

and the Hom-set bijection:

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)). $$
Proof.

By the PST functorial-trajectory axiom,

$$ U=\Phi_z(Q_1), $$ $$ R=\Phi_o(U), $$ $$ I=\Phi_z(R), $$ $$ Q_2=\Phi_o(I). $$

Substituting successively,

$$ R=\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1)), $$ $$ I=\Phi_z(\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1))), $$ $$ Q_2=\Phi_o(\Phi_z(\Phi_o(\Phi_z(Q_1)))). $$

Therefore,

$$ Q_2=(\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z\circ\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z)(Q_1). $$

Thus the PST functor-composition theorem is proved.

By the adjunction \(\Phi_o\dashv\Phi_z\), there exist:

$$ \eta:\operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal P} \Longrightarrow \Phi_z\circ\Phi_o, $$ $$ \varepsilon:\Phi_o\circ\Phi_z \Longrightarrow \operatorname{Id}_{\mathcal O}. $$

Moreover, by the Hom-set characterization of the adjunction,

$$ \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal O}(\Phi_o(P),O) \cong \operatorname{Hom}_{\mathcal P}(P,\Phi_z(O)) $$

for every \(P\in\mathcal P\) and \(O\in\mathcal O\). Thus the final PST categorical theorem is proved.

What is not proved

Remark (What is not proved).

The preceding theorems do not prove the following without additional PST axioms:

  • That every process must have exactly five nodes and four edges.
  • That \(Q_1\) must be raw material and \(Q_2\) must be an autonomous product.
  • That \(R\) must be minimal.
  • That polarity must be \(\mathrm{off}-\mathrm{on}-\mathrm{on}-\mathrm{off}\).
  • That every edge must internally unfold as \(\varepsilon-\eta-\eta-\varepsilon\).
  • That the existence of a Hom-morphism necessarily implies a physical current.

These require separate PST axioms or empirical hypotheses.
